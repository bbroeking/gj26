# Doom 1993

First-person shooter released December 10, 1993 by id Software that invented the deathmatch, the WAD modding system, and the shareware distribution model that bootstrapped online gaming.

## Design

**Core loop.** Navigate sector-based levels on Mars and in Hell, collect keycards, survive enemy ambushes, reach the exit. Five difficulty levels gate enemy density and aggression rather than changing content — an elegant accessibility dial.

**Weapons and resources.** A compact arsenal (pistol, shotgun, chainsaw, rocket launcher, plasma rifle, BFG 9000) governs pacing through ammo scarcity. No reloading; the "Doom loop" is pick-up management, positional awareness, and weapon-switching cadence, not aim-down-sights.

**Deathmatch.** John Romero coined the term on the spot when he first watched two networked players fight. The concept — free-for-all, respawning PvP on shared maps — was invented for Doom, and the vocabulary ("frag," "deathmatch") migrated intact into every FPS that followed.

**Level design philosophy.** Romero designed levels as spatial experiences: height variation, light gradients, monster closets, secret rooms. Levels double as both single-player puzzles and deathmatch arenas without redesign — a rare structural economy.

## Implementation

**Engine — id Tech 1.** John Carmack's 2.5D engine renders a fully 2D floor plan at 3D perspective using binary space partitioning (BSP) for draw-order and palette-based sector lighting for atmosphere. Walls are always vertical; rooms cannot stack; the player cannot look up or down. These constraints freed the renderer to hit 70fps on a 33 MHz 486 — faster than any true 3D rival.

**Game logic tick rate.** Game simulation runs at a fixed 35 Hz regardless of rendering speed, so multiplayer clients across different hardware remain synchronized without a dedicated server.

**Netcode — peer-to-peer, no server.** Each player runs an independent simulation; only input commands are broadcast over IPX (LAN) or modem dial-up. Because every client deterministically replays the same inputs from the same seed, no game-state packets are needed. The cost: modem sessions were laggy and desync-prone, and early IPX code broadcast to the entire LAN, earning Doom network bans in corporate offices.

**Modding — WAD format.** All game data (maps, textures, sounds, sprites) lives in WAD archives separate from the engine binary. Third parties can ship total replacements by supplying a new WAD. This was an architectural decision, not an afterthought, and it launched one of the most sustained modding communities in history.

**Distribution.** id released Episode 1 as shareware — free to copy and distribute. The model generated an estimated $100,000/day at peak. It prefigured the free-to-play funnel by 15 years.

## Why it matters

Doom invented the vocabulary of the FPS genre: deathmatch, frag, WAD, megawad. Its fixed-tick peer-to-peer determinism was a pragmatic solution to 1993 hardware that influenced every subsequent LAN-party game. The shareware model proved that giving away the first act drives more revenue than gating all of it. The WAD system demonstrated that decoupling data from engine multiplies a game's lifespan by orders of magnitude.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **Modding architecture.** WAD's separation of data from engine is the ancestor of Godot's scene/resource system. Wayfinder's [[Charts]] — parameterized dungeon keys that describe a run without being the run — are philosophically the same decoupling applied to procedural content.
- **Fixed-tick determinism.** Doom's 35 Hz fixed-step loop kept peers in sync without a server. Wayfinder's [[Multiplayer Co-op]] design can draw on the same idea: a fixed sim tick shared by all clients reduces drift in a small-session co-op context.
- **Difficulty scaling via density, not content.** Doom's five difficulty levels add monsters, not new maps. Relevant to how [[Charts]] could tune difficulty through Affix density rather than authoring separate dungeons.
