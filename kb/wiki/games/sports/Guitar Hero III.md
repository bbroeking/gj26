# Guitar Hero III

A peripheral-driven rhythm game that became the first retail video game to surpass one billion dollars in sales, cementing the note-highway scrolling pattern as a mainstream template.

## Design

Guitar Hero III's loop is built around a guitar-shaped controller and a scrolling note highway. Colored fret buttons on the neck correspond to five note lanes; the player presses the correct button while strumming the strum bar as each note crosses the strike line. Notes can be sustained (hold the fret), chained into chords (two to four buttons), or bent with a whammy bar during sustains. A performance meter rises with successful hits and drops on misses; depleting it ends the song with the band being booed offstage.

Star Power is the standout meta-mechanic. Starred note sequences fill a Star Power gauge; the player activates it by tilting the guitar vertically or pressing the select button. While active, the combo multiplier doubles (up to 8×) and the performance meter drains more slowly — creating a risk-reward decision about when to burn the gauge. Four difficulty levels (Easy through Expert) compress or expand the note highway's active lanes and increase note density.

Multiplayer introduced Guitar Hero's first online modes: Face-Off (individual scoring on the same song), Co-Op Career (lead + rhythm/bass), and Battle Mode — an adversarial variant where Star Power charges attacks that disrupt the opponent (broken strings, inverted controls, increased note speed). The Boss Battle format added character-based fights against Slash, Tom Morello, and Lou the Devil, each requiring Battle attacks to whittle down the opponent's HP. These framing devices gave what is essentially an abstract scoring game a narrative shape.

## Implementation

Neversoft developed Guitar Hero III after Activision acquired RedOctane and Harmonix departed for Rock Band. The team manually authored hammer-on and pull-off flags on every note — the engine allows sequences of notes to be hit without re-strumming, enabling fluid lead-guitar runs. Wireless Gibson Les Paul replicas shipped as the bundled peripheral for each platform, reducing cable friction that affected stage feel.

Online multiplayer was new to the series; implementing it across PS3, Wii, and Xbox 360 simultaneously required platform-specific network back-ends. The game's 73-track setlist included 54 master recordings — a notable shift from covers — raising licensing complexity but improving authenticity.

## Why it matters

Guitar Hero III demonstrated that a music-peripheral game could achieve blockbuster commercial scale ($1 billion retail, 8 million+ copies by mid-2008) and permanently established the note-highway scrolling paradigm as a genre template. Its note placement and hit window were widely cited as the series' most refined, and the Boss Battle format showed how competition mechanics can imbue a rhythm game with dramatic stakes beyond the score.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

1. → [[Camera and Game Feel]]: The multiplier-on-star-power system is a clean model for juice escalation — a secondary resource the player earns through skill, then activates for a heightened-feedback window. Wayfinder's combat combo meter could follow this pattern.
2. → [[Onboarding and Tutorial]]: Guitar Hero III's four-difficulty lane compression is a textbook progressive disclosure of complexity; Easy hides three of five lanes entirely. Wayfinder's gather and chart systems might onboard with similarly masked complexity rather than front-loading all verbs.
