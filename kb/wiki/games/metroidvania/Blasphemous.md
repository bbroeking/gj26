# Blasphemous

Metroidvania/soulslike hybrid (2019, The Game Kitchen / Team17, PC/console) that replaced traditional ability-gating with player-choice exploration and drew its aesthetic from Spanish Catholic religious art, funded via Kickstarter.

## Design

- **Exploration without hard ability gates:** The Game Kitchen deliberately avoided the classic "acquire new power → open locked door" loop because, as level designer Enrique Colinet stated, "we don't want players to backtrack or explore areas if they don't want to." Instead, progression uses interactable levers, breakable cases, teleport fast-travel, and optional shortcuts.
- **Three-tier world hierarchy:** The world (Cvstodia) is organised into Districts (thematic regions) → Zones → Scenes (individual screens). Switching from scrolling to screen-based design in mid-2018 improved team collaboration and performance.
- **Guided non-linearity:** The early game is linear to teach combat, then opens at the Wasteland into a genuine branch: the hard path ("Where Olive Trees Wither") or the easier "Mercy Dreams." Post-launch patches added visual cues (a stepladder replacing a gap) to nudge players toward the intended entry point without removing the choice.
- **Dark Souls influence on traversal:** Fast-travel shortcuts connecting distant districts — borrowed from Dark Souls bonfires and classic God of War — replace ability-gate backtracking as the primary revisit mechanism.
- **Soulslike combat elements (first game):** Stamina-free but resource-gated combat (Fervor for spells), Tears of Atonement currency lost on death, and parry/counterattack as the high-skill option. *Blasphemous 2* walked back the harsher Souls elements (loss on death, instant-kill hazards) to lean harder into Metroidvania.
- **Checkpoints:** Roughly one per area, never more than seven screens apart — a concrete design constraint that kept pacing consistent.

## Implementation

- Developed by **The Game Kitchen**, a Seville-based indie studio. Creative director Enrique Cabeza drew on Spanish religious iconography and Goya's paintings for the grotesque aesthetic. Kickstarter campaign launched 2017; game shipped September 2019.
- Engine: **Unity**. Published by Team17.
- Reached 1 million players by March 2021 across all platforms; Metacritic 76–82/100. Critics praised the art, combat, and boss design; backtracking and level legibility were the main complaints — both addressed in *Blasphemous 2* (2023).

## Why it matters

Blasphemous is a case study in genre blending as a design risk: it satisfied neither pure Souls fans nor pure Metroidvania fans initially, but found an audience through art direction so distinctive that the aesthetic *became* the brand. Its level-design postmortem is unusually honest about mid-production pivots (the scroll-to-screen switch) and the difficulty of non-linear map authoring ("designing a non-linear map is like being the first person exploring it"). The sequel's course-correction toward cleaner Metroidvania structure shows how player feedback can sharpen a hybrid.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **[[Dungeon Generation]]:** The Districts → Zones → Scenes hierarchy and the screen-based scene model are a useful reference for how to segment and compose dungeon rooms without losing the sense of a contiguous world.
- **[[Combat]]:** Blasphemous' parry-forward combat and stamina-free but resource-gated spell casting is structurally close to Wayfinder's "combat as one verb"; the sequel's lessons about removing punishing Souls elements while keeping skill expression are directly applicable.
- **[[Bosses]]:** The game's boss battles are its most praised content — complex multi-phase encounters tied tightly to the religious-horror aesthetic. Boss design as the primary showcase of world-building is a transferable principle for Wayfinder's trophy-chain endgame.
