# Castlevania Symphony of the Night

Action-RPG (1997, Konami Computer Entertainment Tokyo, PlayStation) that fused Metroid-style exploration with RPG leveling and equipment, coining the second half of "Metroidvania" and defining the genre's RPG branch.

## Design

- **RPG layered onto exploration:** Alucard gains EXP, levels, and equippable weapons/armor that scale stats — not just traversal abilities. This separation means area access can be gated by both ability AND power level, giving designers two independent difficulty levers.
- **Ability-form transformation:** Wolf, mist, and bat transformations unlock traversal options (Super Metroid's power-up model), but are supplemented by relics and familiars that provide passive or toggled bonuses — a richer toolbox than pure ability gates.
- **The inverted castle:** Completing the castle unlocks a fully mirrored second castle — identical geometry, harder enemies, different item drops — extending playtime dramatically with almost zero new art. A pure content-doubling trick.
- **Non-linear hub structure:** The castle is one contiguous interconnected world; no level select. Players travel back and forth, discovering new paths as power grows. Inspired explicitly by *The Legend of Zelda* and *Super Metroid*.
- **Slow early burn → power fantasy:** Early Alucard is deliberately weak; by late game he is overpowered. The RPG stat ramp makes the power fantasy visceral and player-earned.
- **Hidden content density:** False walls, breakable floors, missable items, and secret rooms reward thorough exploration beyond the critical path.

## Implementation

- Directed by Toru Hagihara; Koji Igarashi served as assistant director and contributed programming and story — his first major credit. Igarashi later became the series' face and carried the design philosophy into the "Igavania" sub-lineage.
- The project began life as a cancelled Sega 32X title (*Castlevania: The Bloodletting*), giving the team freedom to break series conventions when it moved to PlayStation.
- Shipped March 1997 (Japan); October 1997 (NA). Sold poorly at launch — initial reviews were mixed partly due to comparisons with *Super Mario 64* — but became a sleeper hit through word-of-mouth, eventually moving 700,000+ units in the US and Japan.
- Metacritic: 93/100 on PS1.

## Why it matters

SotN established that a Metroid-style world gains a second dimension of progression richness when you add RPG stats: exploration now serves *both* spatial and numerical growth. The inverted castle remains the canonical example of content-doubling via geometry reuse. Its slow-start-to-power-fantasy arc is a template for RPG-adjacent action games where player mastery and character power grow together.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **[[Skills]] / [[Items and Gear]]:** The dual gating model (ability gate + stat gate) maps directly to Wayfinder's Trade leveling — a high-tier gather node might require both the right Trade skill (ability) and sufficient gear level to survive the associated enemy.
- **[[Bosses]]:** Trophy-chain unlocking deeper dens is structurally similar to SotN's inverted castle reveal — the player earns a "second layer" by beating the surface layer.
- **[[Dungeon Generation]]:** The contiguous hub-world principle (no level-select seams) is an aspiration for Wayfinder's dungeon stack; procedural generation must preserve the "one connected world" feeling.
