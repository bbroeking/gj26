# My Time at Portia

3D crafting life-sim RPG (2019, Pathea Games / Team17) — a post-apocalyptic workshop-building game set in a sunlit city-state where players restore their late father's workshop by fulfilling commission-board contracts, upgrading crafting stations through a tiered rank system, and exploring Ancient Ruins for rare materials, with town-scale construction projects that visibly reshape the world.

## Design

- **Commission board loop:** The Commerce Guild posts daily commissions — requests from townspeople for crafted items. Accepting and completing commissions earns gold, reputation, and Workshop Rank points. This is the primary progression spine: commissions pull players into crafting, which pulls them into gathering, which pulls them into Ruins exploration. Major story commissions (build a bridge, construct a bus stop) physically unlock new map areas.
- **Workshop Rank system:** The Commerce Guild ranks all workshops in Portia from lowest to highest tier. Rank gates which commissions players can accept (harder commissions require more advanced crafting stations and materials) and determines end-of-month bonus rewards. The top three workshops receive special prizes in a monthly ceremony — a light competitive framing that motivates consistent daily engagement without punishing casual players.
- **Crafting station progression:** Players upgrade their workshop with increasingly complex machines (Furnace → Industrial Furnace → Recycler). Each station unlocks new recipe tiers. Progression is horizontal as well as vertical — multiple station types (smelting, cutting, assembly, cooking) run simultaneously, and late-game optimization is about managing queue times across parallel machines.
- **Ancient Ruins exploration:** Two Ruins types: standard mines (grid-based excavation for common ores and relics) and Hazardous Ruins (dungeon crawlers with real-time combat, rare electronic components, boss enemies). Relics discovered in the mines can be assembled into furniture or submitted to the Research Center for data points. Ruins function as Wayfinder-style "delves" that supply materials unavailable through surface gathering.
- **NPC relationships and town growth:** 30+ named NPCs with gift preferences, schedules, and dialogue trees. Relationship levels unlock heart events and eventually romance/marriage options. Unlike Stardew Valley, NPC relationships feel secondary to the crafting loop — critics noted the cast was "charming" but less emotionally layered. Town growth (new shops open, new NPCs arrive, infrastructure completes) provides visible world-change feedback for player labor.
- **Pacing problems:** The most-cited design flaw. Late-game crafting queues require players to wake up, refill machines, then literally go back to sleep to pass time. A reviewer who played 70 hours noted excessive machine-babysitting and wished crafting times were halved. Dungeon content unlocks too slowly relative to the crafting downtime it would fill.

## Implementation

- **Developer/publisher:** Pathea Games (Chongqing, China) developed; Team17 published. My Time at Portia was Pathea's debut major title, developed by a small team. The game ran in Steam Early Access from January 2018 before the January 2019 1.0 release.
- **Engine:** Unity. The 3D open-world with day/night cycle, weather, and real-time combat was Pathea's first Unity project at this scale.
- **Save model:** Per-file saves with autosave at day-end; console versions limited save slots. No cloud save on initial release.
- **Commercial performance:** Steam's best-selling new release in January 2019. Metacritic scores: PC 72, Switch 71, PS4 75. Critics praised the world charm and crafting variety while flagging pacing.
- **Sequel/successor:** *My Time at Sandrock* (November 2023) addressed most Portia pacing critiques — faster machine times, earlier dungeon access. *My Time at Evershine* is in development as of 2026.

## Why it matters

My Time at Portia demonstrated that the **commission-board as progression driver** works as a cozy alternative to pure seasonal farming — it shifts the question from "when do I harvest?" to "what do I build next?" The visible town transformation (bridges appear, NPCs move in, new districts open) creates the most visceral sense of **player impact on a world** of any game in this cluster. Its failures are equally instructive: machine-queue downtime without meaningful activity is the enemy of cozy pacing, and dungeon content gated too far into mid-game leaves a crafting vacuum. The sequel Sandrock shows those lessons were learned.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **Commission board = Wayfinder's chart request economy:** The commission-board structure — daily pull of varied contracts, fulfillment via crafting → gathering → exploration — is the closest analogue to how Wayfinder's chart loop could generate daily play motivation. Charts as "commissions" inscribed by the Wayfinder trade make literal narrative sense — see [[Trades and Leveling]] and [[Economy]].
- **Town transformation as skilling payoff:** Major Portia commissions visibly reshape the city. Wayfinder's Bramblewood could follow suit — Trophy submissions or Trade milestones that add new NPCs, open new market stalls, or unlock new areas give cozy skilling a spatial payoff beyond stat bars — see [[NPCs]] and [[Voice and Tone]].
- **Pacing anti-pattern — avoid machine babysitting:** Portia's late-game downtime is a cautionary tale. Wayfinder's crafting must avoid "queue and sleep" loops; gather channels with active minigames and crafting that completes while the player is in a dungeon run keep both modes mutually reinforcing rather than one blocking the other — see [[Crafting]] and [[Balance Philosophy]].
