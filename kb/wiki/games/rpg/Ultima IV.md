# Ultima IV

Landmark CRPG (1985, Origin Systems / Richard Garriott) that replaced kill-the-villain goal-structure with a virtue-based moral journey — the player's only objective is to become the Avatar by embodying Eight Virtues — making it the first major RPG to embed ethics directly into its progression loop.

## Design

- **No villain, no treasure: virtue is the goal.** Previous Ultima games tasked players with defeating an antagonist; Ultima IV has no final boss. Instead, the player works toward Avatarhood — mastering Honesty, Compassion, Valor, Justice, Sacrifice, Honor, Spirituality, and Humility — by genuinely practicing them in the world, not just by completing quests. Giving gold to beggars, letting non-evil enemies flee, never stealing, and answering truthfully all accumulate virtue in the relevant discipline. Failing NPCs' questions or cheating in combat sets progress back.
- **Character class from ethical self-assessment.** Rather than assigning stats at character creation, the player answers a series of moral-dilemma vignettes — "you find a wounded enemy: do you help or press your advantage?" — and the game derives the starting class from the pattern of answers. Virtue priorities map directly to the eight classes (Mage → Honesty, Paladin → Honor, Bard → Compassion, etc.).
- **Interactive NPC dialogue.** Ultima IV was the first in the series to allow players to type conversation keywords to NPCs and receive contextually relevant responses, rather than receiving one canned reply per NPC. This made the world feel like a place of information and social consequence rather than a set-dressing backdrop.
- **Eight companions, one per virtue.** A companion tied to each virtue can join the party, reinforcing the moral architecture in the party-building mechanic itself.
- **Shrine meditation and the Abyss.** Progress is made visible by meditating at virtue shrines with the correct mantra and the appropriate number of party members. The game culminates in a descent into the Abyss, where the Codex of Ultimate Wisdom poses virtue questions to confirm Avatarhood — the dungeon crawl is a moral exam.

## Implementation

- **Apple II engine, 1985.** Built by Richard Garriott on the Apple II (with rapid ports to C64, Atari 8-bit, DOS, NES, and FM Towns). The world was 16x larger than Ultima III. The virtue-tracking system was implemented as discrete counters per virtue, modified by player actions across the open world — a lightweight reputation system by modern standards, but groundbreaking for 1985.
- **Shift driven by external pressure.** Garriott later stated that letters from parents concerned about his games' emphasis on theft and violence prompted the design pivot. The result became the genre's founding document for ethical CRPGs. Sold over 400,000 copies by 1989.

## Why it matters

Ultima IV proved that a progression loop could be structured around *who the player becomes* rather than *what the player defeats*. The virtue system is the earliest canonical example of a morality loop as the game's central axis — influencing every subsequent CRPG that tracks ethical choices (Planescape: Torment, Fallout, Disco Elysium). The class-from-questions character creation is the ancestor of every "answer these scenarios to find your archetype" design pattern.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **[[Trades and Leveling]]:** The virtue loop — observable actions accumulate into identity — directly models how Wayfinder's Trade advancement could feel earned: gathering ore honestly accumulates Earthcraft not because of XP numbers but because that *is* what earthcrafters do. The insight: progression as practiced behavior, not combat yield.
- **[[Voice and Tone]]:** Britannia's "noble aspirations" tone is a cozy-adjacent register — the world rewards goodness and curiosity, not power. Bramblewood shares that aspiration; Garriott's framing of the Avatar as "the example everyone looks up to" is useful fuel for how Wayfinder's characters talk about the player.
- **[[Skills]]:** The eight-companion system (one per virtue) hints at a design pattern Wayfinder could adopt: companions who embody Trades, deepening the player's engagement with a discipline through relationship, not just practice.
