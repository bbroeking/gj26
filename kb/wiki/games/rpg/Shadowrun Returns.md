# Shadowrun Returns

Tactical cyberpunk-fantasy RPG (2013, Harebrained Schemes) reviving the Shadowrun tabletop license with a Kickstarter-funded isometric adaptation — notable for a classless karma progression system, turn-based tactical combat, and a tone that demonstrated small studios could build dense genre hybrids from IP without publisher backing.

## Design

- **Karma as a classless skill currency.** There are no character levels in the traditional sense. Players earn Karma by completing runs and spend it freely across any skill or attribute — Body, Quickness, Strength, Intelligence, Willpower, Charisma, and Magic — plus sub-skills in weapons, cyberware, and the Matrix. Six archetypes at character creation (Street Samurai, Mage, Shaman, Decker, Rigger, Physical Adept) simply pre-spend opening Karma; they impose no class lock. A player can start as a Street Samurai and gradually acquire Shaman abilities. The system prioritizes fiction-appropriate hybrid builds: in the Shadowrun universe, runners are generalists.
- **Archetype roles with distinct tactical identities.** Despite classlessness, archetypes have sharply defined combat roles. Deckers jack into the Matrix during runs to disable security systems, unlock doors, and disable enemy drones — they fight a parallel tactical fight in cyberspace. Shamans summon spirits (animal-totem themed: Rat, Wolf, Eagle) for combat support and healing; they are the primary support class. Riggers project consciousness into drones. Mages cast direct-damage spells. Street Samurai and Physical Adepts are the melee and ranged combat specialists. Each archetype requires investment to function — you can't split Karma between Decking and Spellcasting and be competent at both.
- **Turn-based tactical combat with action points.** Each character gets two Action Points per turn used for movement, shooting, spellcasting, or ability use. Cover matters; flanking removes it. Encounters are authored set-pieces rather than random-generated — each fight is placed in a specific narrative context. Combat is fast (most encounters last 4–8 turns) and lethal.
- **Linear narrative with meaningful NPCs.** The story follows a dead man's message into a murder investigation uncovering insect-spirit possession in Seattle. It is largely linear, but NPC "Hirelings" (mercenaries purchased between runs) allow squad customization that indirectly shapes playstyle. The moral choices offered are few but legible — the game doesn't try to simulate an open moral space.
- **Editor and modding pipeline.** Harebrained shipped a bundled editor allowing players to build their own campaigns ("Dead Man's Switch" is the shipped campaign; player campaigns like "Dragonfall" began as mods). Dragonfall (2014) and Hong Kong (2015) expanded the IP with richer narrative and systems.

## Implementation

- **Unity engine, Kickstarter origin.** Raised $1.89 million against a $400,000 goal in April 2012 — met within 28 hours. Released July 2013. Metacritic 76/100. The save-system limitation (saves only at area transitions, not mid-run) was the most-criticized design choice and was not fully resolved in sequels. Ported to Switch/PS/Xbox in 2022.
- **Harebrained Schemes** was co-founded by Jordan Weisman, original creator of the Shadowrun tabletop RPG, ensuring setting authenticity. The studio was later acquired by Paradox Interactive (2018).

## Why it matters

Shadowrun Returns proved that **classless karma spend + locked archetype roles** is a viable middle path between full freedom (Ultima IV's virtue-as-identity) and rigid class systems (D&D). The design lets players define a build philosophy without infinite respec anxiety: you can drift, but drift has costs. It also demonstrated that a small team could produce a satisfying genre hybrid from licensed IP via crowdfunding — validating the Kickstarter CRPG wave that followed (Wasteland 2, Pillars of Eternity, Torment: Tides of Numenera).

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **[[Trades and Leveling]]:** The karma + archetype model directly parallels how Wayfinder's Trades could feel: free-spend points within a discipline context, where Earthcraft and Wildcraft offer distinct tactical identities even though both characters earn the same XP currency. The lesson: classlessness needs archetype *flavor* to feel meaningful.
- **[[Skills]]:** The Decker's parallel Matrix combat (fighting a different game inside the same encounter) is an interesting reference for how Wayfinder's Wayfinding trade could feel distinct in dungeon encounters — the Wayfinder inscribes charts that shape the run; that's a cognitive layer others don't have.
- **[[Voice and Tone]]:** Shadowrun's cyberpunk-fantasy hybrid tone (gritty streets + animal shamanism + corporate megastructures) is the aesthetic opposite of Bramblewood's cozy fairytale — but the *commitment* to setting idiom is instructive. Every system in Shadowrun returns has Shadowrun flavor: karma, runs, fixers, the Matrix. Wayfinder's equivalents (charts, trades, the Wayfinding) should be equally saturated with Bramblewood language.
