# Torment Tides of Numenera

Narrative isometric RPG (2017, inXile Entertainment) serving as the thematic spiritual successor to Planescape: Torment — set in Monte Cook's far-future Numenera world, built around a five-color Tides alignment system and the "Crisis" combat-replacement mechanic, emphasizing that most encounters should be solvable without violence.

## Design

- **The Tides: alignment as legacy, not morality.** Rather than good/evil or law/chaos, Tides of Numenera tracks five color-coded emotional orientations — Blue (wisdom/thought), Red (passion/action), Indigo (justice/duty), Gold (sacrifice/charity), Silver (power/prestige) — as invisible forces that accumulate from the player's choices. The dominant Tide shapes the player-character's "Legacy": how they are remembered and how NPCs with matching Tides respond. Matching a Tide can unlock dialogue options, ease skill checks, or cause NPCs to trust or distrust the player character without knowing exactly why. It is explicitly not a morality meter; Red/Silver (passionate and power-driven) are not coded as evil.
- **Crises: combat as a last resort.** Combat occurs only within "Crises," a distinct turn-based mode sharing the same skill-check system as the rest of the game. In a Crisis, options include fighting, fleeing, conversing, using items on objects, manipulating the environment, or finding a non-lethal resolution path. A Numenera device that emits light can solve the fight; a dialogue check can convince the aggressor to stand down; a lockpicking action mid-Crisis can open an escape route. The design intention is that violence is never the *only* option and is never mechanically privileged over alternatives.
- **Narrative character creation.** Rather than assigning stats and picking a backstory, character creation in Tides of Numenera unfolds through a series of narrative questions ("how did you survive your first death?") that allocate starting stats and abilities organically. Three base classes (Glaive = physical, Nano = arcane/tech, Jack = hybrid) are modified by descriptors (Clever, Graceful, Strong) and foci (Fights with Two Weapons, Howls at the Moon) that produce compound character identities: "I am a Clever Nano who Talks to Machines."
- **Deep world legibility.** The Ninth World (Earth one billion years in the future) is layered with technological ruins called "numenera" that appear magical to inhabitants who lack the context to explain them. The game rewards curiosity: strange devices can be understood through Lore skills, and understanding them often unlocks resolution paths unavailable to players who ignore the setting's lore.
- **Personal identity as the through-line.** The central question the game poses — echoing Planescape: Torment's "What can change the nature of a man?" — is "What does one life matter?" The player is the Last Castoff, one of many bodies discarded by an immortal known as the Changing God. The narrative is an investigation of selfhood, memory, and legacy rather than an external threat.

## Implementation

- **Unity engine, 2.5D isometric pre-rendered art.** Released February 28, 2017 for PC, macOS, Linux, PS4, and Xbox One. Metacritic 81/100 PC, 76/100 PS4. The game raised $4,188,927 on Kickstarter in 2013 — the highest Kickstarter-funded video game at that time — plus $870,000 post-campaign. The development stretched from 2013 to 2017 (four years), longer than backers expected, due to scope expansion.
- **Combat mode was decided by fan vote.** During development, inXile asked backers to choose between real-time-with-pause (Baldur's Gate style) and turn-based. Turn-based won; the result was the Crisis system. This is a rare case of a design pillar decided by crowdfunding audience vote.

## Why it matters

Tides of Numenera is the definitive modern case study for **alignment as behavioral legacy rather than moral judgment**. The Tides system solves a long-standing RPG problem: good/evil alignment flattens character motivation into a score. The Tides track *how* a character acts (with wisdom, passion, justice, sacrifice, or ambition), not *whether* they're good or bad — and that coloring shapes the social texture of the world without railroading. The Crisis system proves that turn-based combat can be richly narrative when combat resolution is one option among many.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- **[[Trades and Leveling]]:** The Tides' accumulation through *pattern of behavior* rather than explicit choice is directly applicable to how a Wayfinder's Trade identity could emerge: a player who consistently chooses the more curious/careful/collaborative resolution in dungeon encounters could accumulate a "Wayfinder mark" that opens dialogue options others don't see. Trade identity as behavioral legacy, not just XP spent.
- **[[Skills]]:** The Nano's "I am a Clever Nano who Talks to Machines" compound identity (class + descriptor + focus) is a clean model for how Wayfinder's hotbar skills could communicate character voice — a Wildcraft skill named and flavored for a player who has taken specific Trade milestones feels meaningfully different from the base version.
- **[[Voice and Tone]]:** The Ninth World's layered strangeness (magic = misunderstood technology, ruins = wonder) shares Bramblewood's sense that the world contains more than it reveals. Both worlds reward players who look closely. The tone design principle: every object and NPC in Bramblewood should imply a larger world; the crypt has history before the player arrived.
