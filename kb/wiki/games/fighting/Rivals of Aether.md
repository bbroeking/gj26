# Rivals of Aether

Indie platform-fighter franchise by Dan Fornace / Aether Studios — the original (2017) stripped the genre to its offensive core by removing shields, edge-grabs, and ledges in favour of a timed-parry system and elemental stage control; the sequel (2024) restored those conventions while preserving the franchise's mechanical identity and commitment to competitive-quality rollback netcode.

## Design

**Original (Rivals of Aether, 2017).** Released on Windows in March 2017, Xbox One in August 2017, and Nintendo Switch in September 2020. The base roster features 16 characters organised around classical elements (fire, water, air, earth), plus licensed guest fighters Ori (Ori and the Blind Forest) and Shovel Knight. The defining design choice: no shields, no ledge-grabs. Blocking is replaced by a precise timed parry, forcing players into an aggressive, punish-heavy game with no safe defensive option to fall back on. Every character also has at least one move that leaves a lasting environmental effect — ice patches, bubble traps, fire zones — creating dynamic, shifting stage geometry unique to each matchup. Monetization is a paid purchase with optional cosmetic skins; no F2P element. The game subsequently added official support for four community-created characters, unusually embracing a quasi-open-roster model.

**Sequel (Rivals of Aether II, 2024).** Released October 23, 2024 on Windows via Steam at $30. Published jointly by Aether Studios and Offbrand Games (co-founded by YouTube streamer Ludwig Ahgren, who also joined as a creative lead). Roster launched with 10 characters, expanded to 16 by April 2026; Aether Studios commits to ~4 free post-launch fighters per year — all future characters are free updates. The sequel adopts 2.5D visuals powered by Unreal Engine 5 and reintegrates the mechanics the original deliberately removed: shields, shield breaks, ledge interactions, grabs (bypassing shields), getup options, and pummels. The parry system is retained. One character (Slade) can shop for items and go fishing mid-match, signalling the game's tolerance for eccentric outliers within a tight competitive frame. No story mode shipped at launch; co-op details are not prominent in competitive coverage. Cosmetics unlock through character leveling (up to level 99) or permanent shop purchases with no FOMO-gating — a model the review community praised as "you can never gain an advantage by paying real money."

## Implementation

**Rollback netcode (original).** Aether Studios added rollback netcode to the original Rivals of Aether in February 2022, years after launch — a retroactive upgrade that meaningfully improved the online competitive experience. The implementation arrived alongside four officially adopted fan-made characters, suggesting a tight-knit developer–community relationship.

**Rivals of Aether II.** The sequel launched with rollback netcode from day one, built on Unreal Engine 5's networking infrastructure. Reviewers and the competitive community have not flagged notable netcode issues, suggesting the implementation is solid. Steam Workshop mod support from the original is absent in the sequel (at least at launch), narrowing the community-creation surface area.

## Why it matters

The Rivals franchise makes a strong argument that principled constraint is a design tool: stripping shields and ledges from the original forced a distinctive, high-agency fighting system that influenced a generation of indie platform-fighter designers. The sequel then tests the inverse question — whether restoring genre conventions can coexist with that identity, rather than dissolving it. The commitment to free post-launch characters (four per year) in the sequel is one of the more player-friendly monetization models in the genre. Ludwig's involvement as a co-publisher also demonstrates a streamer-to-game-developer pipeline that broadened the franchise's cultural footprint.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

1. → [[Combat]]: the elemental stage-control mechanic — moves that alter the battlefield geometry mid-fight — is directly relevant to how Wayfinder could make dungeon rooms feel reactive and dynamic rather than static arenas; environmental hazards and persistent spell effects share the same design logic.
2. → [[Design Influences]]: the "principled removal" philosophy (no shields, no ledges) is a useful frame for evaluating which mechanics Wayfinder's one-verb combat should cut rather than include; constraints that force engagement can elevate feel.
3. → [[Multiplayer Co-op]]: the free post-launch roster model and no-FOMO cosmetic system are strong references for how Wayfinder might handle post-launch content without pricing out the cozy audience.
