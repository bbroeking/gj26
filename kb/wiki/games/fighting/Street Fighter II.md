# Street Fighter II

1991 arcade fighting game by Capcom that invented the conventions — six-button layout, special moves, competitive combos — every head-to-head fighter has used since.

## Design

- **Six-button layout:** three punches and three kicks gave players a wide verb space while remaining learnable; the scheme became the genre default.
- **Special moves via motion inputs:** quarter-circle and charge motions encoded power into physical skill, making victories feel earned rather than random.
- **Combos — accidental then sacred:** combo chaining was initially a bug in the CPS-1 animation logic; Capcom found it and left it in as a "secret technique" for skilled players, birthing the entire frame-data subculture.
- **Roster of archetypes:** eight characters with distinct hitboxes, ranges, and specials established the archetype model (grappler, zoner, rushdown) still referenced in every fighter design doc.
- **Tournament DNA:** the game displaced high-score chasing with face-to-face skill tests, directly seeding what became the Evolution Championship Series (EVO).

## Implementation

- **Hardware:** Capcom's CP System (CPS-1) arcade board; 16 MHz 68000 CPU with a Z80 sound co-processor and custom sprite hardware capable of large, detailed fighter sprites.
- **No online — no netcode:** the 1991 arcade era predated networked play entirely. Lagless local competition was the implicit standard; later HD ports added delay-based online that the community has long criticized.
- **Rollback context:** Street Fighter II itself has no rollback story. Its relevance is as the baseline: the genre it defined assumed physical co-location, and every netcode debate since (GGPO, Strive's in-house rollback, Tekken's controversy) is about closing that gap.

## Why it matters

Street Fighter II is the Cambrian explosion of competitive gaming: it invented the genre vocabulary, created the archetype taxonomy, and spawned the tournament ecosystem. Every fighting game in this index is either following or reacting to its blueprints. The accidental-combo discovery also established an enduring design principle — emergent depth from interaction, not from designers scripting every outcome.

## Relevance to Wayfinder

- [[Combat]]: one-verb depth. SF2 demonstrated that a small, precise action space can generate enormous strategic depth — relevant to Wayfinder's "combat as one verb" philosophy where a single fight action needs to feel weighty and readable, not complicated.
- [[Camera and Game Feel]]: readable hit feedback. SF2's screen-shake, freeze-frames on clean hits, and large sprites set the bar for communicating impact — lessons for Wayfinder's hit-feel tuning even in a top-down FATE camera.
- [[Multiplayer Co-op]]: PvP vs. co-op framing. SF2 is purely adversarial; Wayfinder flips the model to co-op delves, but the roster-archetype thinking (each player fills a distinct role/range) carries over to party composition design.
